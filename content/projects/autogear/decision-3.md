*Problem.* The approach planned during the project classified each second as shift down, hold or shift up: two stages for the imbalance (riders shift on 2.6 % of seconds), a raw 30-second window, boosted trees. In closed-loop replay it agrees with the rider within one gear only 66.0 % of the time, below a table that holds a constant cadence (78.8 %). Class weights distort its threshold, shift labels are noisy, and since it sees its own gear and cadence, its errors compound once it leaves the rider's trajectory.

*Choice.* Regress instead the development of the gear the rider will want 3 s ahead, from 14 inputs that do not depend on the engaged gear, and leave the timing to a hysteresis controller: dead band h = 0.6, persistence k = 3 s, one step at a time.

*Why.* The target exists at every second, so the imbalance leaves the learning problem and becomes two controller parameters; timing ambiguity only matters near a gear boundary, where the dead band absorbs it; and the learned part sits outside the closed loop. The formulation carries most of the gain: a 172-byte linear regression of the same target already beats every decision table on 12 of 14 tests.
